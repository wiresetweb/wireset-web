How to get more Google reviews without being annoying

Reviews are the currency of local search. Here’s a simple, no-pressure system to get more of them — and what never to do.

Local SEO June 11, 2026 · 6 min read · By Wireset Web

Reviews do two jobs at once: they help you rank in local search, and they're the first thing a customer reads before deciding to call. Most happy customers would leave one — they just never get asked, or asking feels awkward. Here's how to fix both.

Ask at the moment of relief

Timing is everything. The best moment is right after you've solved their problem — the heat's back on, the car's fixed, the leak's stopped. That's when goodwill peaks. Wait a week and the feeling fades. Ask in person if you can ("Would you mind leaving us a quick review? It really helps."), then make the follow-through effortless.

Remove every ounce of friction

"Look us up on Google" loses most people. Instead, hand them a direct link straight to your review form — a short link, a QR code on the invoice, a one-tap button in your follow-up text. The fewer steps between "sure" and a posted review, the more you'll get.

The lines you don’t cross

A few things will get you in trouble — with customers and with Google:

  • Never buy or fake reviews. It's against Google's rules, it's obvious, and it can get your profile penalized. One fake review isn't worth your credibility.
  • Don't gate reviews. Asking only happy customers while diverting unhappy ones is against the rules and tends to backfire.
  • Don't spam the ask. One genuine request, maybe one gentle reminder. That's it.

Reply to the ones you get — all of them

Responding to reviews, good and bad, shows future customers there's a real person who cares. Thank the happy ones. For a bad one, stay calm, take it offline, and fix it — a gracious reply to a complaint often impresses readers more than a wall of five stars.

You don’t need a hundred reviews. You need a steady trickle of real ones, and the nerve to ask.

Ask at the moment of maximum happiness

Here's the part most people get wrong: they batch up their review asks and fire them off in one awkward email blast at the end of the month. By then the customer barely remembers you. The fix is to ask while they're still smiling. Right after the job goes well — the AC kicks back on, the drain runs clear, the cracked screen looks new — that's your window. Ask in person if you're standing there, or send a text the same day. The wording matters far less than the timing. A clumsy ask at the right moment beats a polished one a week too late. Train yourself (and your crew) to treat "job done, customer happy" as the cue. It becomes a habit fast.

Make the link one tap

Every extra step between "sure, I'll leave a review" and a posted review costs you reviews. "Search for us on Google and scroll down to the reviews part" loses most people before they start. So don't make them search. Grab your Google review short link — it looks like g.page/r/… and points straight to the box where they type and hit five stars. You generate it once inside your Google Business Profile, under the "Ask for reviews" option. (If your profile isn't fully set up yet, fix that first — we cover the common slip-ups in Google Business Profile: the free tool most shops set up wrong.)

Then put that link everywhere a happy customer might be: in a same-day follow-up text, at the bottom of the invoice, as a QR code on a little card you hand over at the end of the job. One tap, and they're typing. The harder it is, the fewer you get — it really is that simple.

A simple ask that doesn’t feel gross

You don't need a sales pitch. You need an honest, low-pressure sentence. In person, something like: "If you were happy with how it went, would you mind leaving us a quick Google review? It helps other folks around here find us." By text, keep it just as short: "Thanks again for today, [name]. If you've got 30 seconds, a quick Google review would mean a lot — it helps other locals find us. [link]" That's it. No begging, no five-paragraph essay, no "we'd really appreciate a 5-star rating." Mentioning that it helps other people in the area is the honest truth and it gives folks a reason that isn't just "do me a favor." Ask once. If nothing comes, one gentle reminder is fine. After that, let it go.

Respond to every review — even the bad ones

Replying to reviews is the most-skipped, highest-leverage thing you can do. It tells every future customer reading along that there's a real human behind the business who actually pays attention. Thank the good ones briefly and by name. The negative ones are where the real opportunity hides. Don't argue, don't get defensive, don't explain why they're wrong. Stay calm, acknowledge it, and take the fix offline: "I'm sorry this didn't go the way it should have, [name]. That's not how we do things — I'd like to make it right. Can you call me at [number]?" Readers don't expect you to be perfect. They're watching how you handle a problem. A gracious reply to a rough review can win you more business than a wall of flawless five stars ever will.

What NOT to do

A few shortcuts look tempting and will quietly cost you. Skip all of them:

  • Don't gate reviews. Sending happy customers to Google while filtering unhappy ones off to a private form is against Google's policies — and it makes your rating a lie that careful shoppers can smell.
  • Don't buy reviews. Fake reviews are obvious, and Google can remove them or penalize your profile. One bought review isn't worth your credibility.
  • Don't pay or discount for reviews. Offering a discount, a freebie, or a raffle entry in exchange for a review violates Google's rules — even if the review is honest. Ask for the review; never trade for it.

The white-hat version isn't slower, either. Ask happy people, at the right time, with an easy link. That's the whole game. The short version

  • Ask right after you’ve solved the problem — goodwill peaks there
  • Hand over a direct link or QR code; never make people search
  • Never buy, fake, or gate reviews — it backfires and breaks Google’s rules
  • Reply to every review, especially the negative ones
  • A steady trickle of genuine reviews beats a one-time pile
