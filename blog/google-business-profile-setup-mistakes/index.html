Google Business Profile: the free tool most shops set up wrong

It's free, it outranks your own website for your business name, and most local businesses leave half of it blank. Here's how to fix that in an afternoon.

Local SEO June 11, 2026 · 7 min read · By Wireset Web

Before anyone sees your website, they see your Google Business Profile — the box with your name, hours, photos, and reviews that pops up on Google and Maps. It's free, it's often the very first impression you make, and most local businesses fill out about half of it and walk away.

That's a mistake, because for a lot of searches your profile is the storefront. People decide whether to call you straight from that box, without ever clicking through. Here's how to make it earn its keep.

Claim it, then actually finish it

Google fills in a basic profile for many businesses automatically — which means there may be one out there you've never logged into. Claim it (search your business name, look for "Own this business?"), then complete every field. The ones most people skip are the ones that win you customers:

  • Categories. Your primary category is one of the strongest signals Google has. Pick the most specific one that fits, then add secondary categories for everything else you do.
  • Services and service areas. List them out. This is free real estate to tell Google exactly what you do and where.
  • Hours — including holidays. A "might be closed" label loses you the call.
  • Photos. Real ones: your truck, your team, finished work. Profiles with photos get noticeably more clicks and direction requests.

Use the parts everyone ignores

The profile has tools that quietly do a lot of work:

  • Posts. Like little announcements — a seasonal offer, a new service, a finished job. They keep the profile looking alive.
  • Q&A. Anyone can ask a question on your profile — and anyone can answer. Seed it yourself with the questions you get all day ("Do you offer free estimates?") so the right answer is the one people see.
  • Products / services with prices. Even a range beats a blank.
  • Messaging. Only turn it on if you'll actually check it — a slow reply here is worse than no button at all.

Point it at a website that backs it up

Your profile and your website work as a pair. The profile gets you found; the website closes the deal. If your profile links to a slow, outdated site — or no site at all — you're sending hard-won attention somewhere that loses it. A fast, mobile-ready site with matching name, address, and phone number reinforces everything your profile is telling Google.

Picking the wrong primary category

This is the one most people get lazy about, and it quietly costs them the most. Your primary category is the single biggest lever you have over what searches you show up for. Pick "General Contractor" when you're really a roofer, and you're competing in a giant, vague pool instead of ranking for the thing people actually type.

So get specific. If you install and repair AC units, "HVAC Contractor" beats "Contractor." If you do mobile detailing, choose the detailing category, not "Car Wash." Google has a long, oddly specific list — start typing and see what comes up. The narrowest accurate option almost always wins.

Then use secondary categories for the rest of what you do. A plumber who also does drain cleaning and water heaters should add those as secondaries. Your primary tells Google your main game; secondaries widen the net without watering down your strongest signal. Don't stuff it with things you don't really do — that backfires — but don't leave money on the table either.

Skipping photos and never adding new ones

A blank photo section reads as "out of business" to a lot of people. A profile with real, current pictures reads as a working shop that takes pride in its work. That difference shows up in calls and direction requests.

Two rules. First, no stock photos. People can smell a generic handshake-and-hard-hat image from a mile away, and it makes you look like every other faceless outfit. Use your actual truck, your actual crew, your actual before-and-afters. Second, keep adding them. A steady trickle of fresh job photos — even a few a month, shot on your phone — tells Google and customers that you're active and busy. Snap a couple shots when you wrap a job and upload them that week. That's the whole system.

Leaving reviews unanswered

Reviews aren't a one-way street. When you reply — to the glowing ones and the rough ones — you show up as a real owner who's paying attention. A short "Thanks, Maria, glad we could get that leak handled fast" does more for the next reader than the star count alone. And on a bad review, a calm, specific reply ("Sorry we missed that window — we've fixed our scheduling so it won't happen again") often impresses the people reading more than the complaint hurts you.

Replying is only half of it, though. You also need a steady stream of new reviews coming in, because a wall of recent ones beats a handful from two years ago. We walk through a no-pressure way to ask in how to get more Google reviews without being annoying — pair that with replying to every one, and your profile starts doing real selling.

Ignoring the free tools: Services, Products, Q&A, and Posts

Most shops fill out the basics and stop. But there are four free sections sitting right there that almost nobody touches — and they're free visibility, which is the best kind.

  • Services. Don't just list names. Add a sentence of description and a price or range to each one. "Drain cleaning — camera inspection included, $149 flat" tells Google what you do and tells the customer what they'll pay before they even call.
  • Products. Even service businesses can use these as little visual cards for packages or popular jobs. They show up with photos and stand out.
  • Q&A. Anyone can ask or answer here, so beat them to it. Seed it with the questions you field every day — "Do you offer free estimates?" "Are you licensed and insured?" — and answer them yourself, clearly.
  • Posts. Treat these like a free bulletin board. A seasonal offer, a finished job, a "booking two weeks out" note. They expire, so post something every couple of weeks to keep the profile looking alive.

Inconsistent name, address, and phone across the web

This is the free SEO worth doing yourself. Your business name, address, and phone number — the "NAP" — needs to be identical everywhere it appears: your website, your Google profile, Yelp, Facebook, old directory listings, all of it. "Main St" in one place and "Main Street" in another, an old phone number on a directory you forgot about — those little mismatches make Google less sure you're one real, trustworthy business, and they send a customer to the wrong number.

So spend an afternoon and chase down every listing with your business name. Make them match, character for character, the version on your website. This is exactly the kind of work that agencies bill you monthly to "manage" — and it's something you can knock out once and mostly forget. The short version

  • Claim your profile and complete every field — especially categories and services
  • Add real photos; they measurably increase calls and direction requests
  • Use Posts and Q&A — seed the questions you already get all day
  • Keep your name, address, and phone identical everywhere online
  • Link to a fast site that backs up the first impression
