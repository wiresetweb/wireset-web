The 7 things every local service homepage needs above the fold

What a customer has to see in the first three seconds — before they scroll, and before they bounce to your competitor.

Websites June 11, 2026 · 7 min read · By Wireset Web

"Above the fold" is the part of your homepage a visitor sees before scrolling. On a phone, that's a small window — and it's where the decision to stay or leave gets made in about three seconds. Here's what has to be in it.

The seven non-negotiables

  • 1. Who you are and what you do. In plain words, instantly. "Northgate Heating & Air — HVAC repair & install." Not a clever slogan that makes them guess. A visitor should never have to scroll, click, or think to figure out what you sell. If your headline could belong to a law firm or a bakery, it's too vague.
  • 2. Where you work. Your city or service area, visible immediately. Local customers need to know they're in the right place before anything else. "Serving Tacoma and the South Sound" tells them in four words whether you're worth their time — and it quietly helps you rank for the searches that matter.
  • 3. A way to contact you, right now. A tap-to-call button or a quote button — not buried in a menu, not three scrolls down. On a phone, a phone number that isn't a tappable link is a tiny act of cruelty. Make calling you the easiest thing on the screen.
  • 4. One reason to trust you. Years in business, a license number, "family-owned," a star rating. One credibility signal up top does a lot of work. People are deciding whether to let a stranger into their home or hand over a deposit — give them a reason to believe you're real and you're good.
  • 5. A clear next step. One obvious action you want them to take, styled so it's impossible to miss. Two buttons fighting for attention is the same as zero. Pick the one thing — call, or get a quote — and make it the loudest element on the page.
  • 6. It loads fast. The best above-the-fold content is worthless if it shows up after a four-second wait. Most people on a phone won't sit through a slow load on a cellular connection — they'll hit back and tap the next result, which is your competitor.
  • 7. It works on a phone. Most local searches happen on mobile. If it's only right on a desktop, it's wrong where it counts. We dig into exactly how to see your site the way customers do in our mobile-first guide.

What does not belong up there

Just as important is what you leave out. The top of your homepage is the most valuable space you own — don't waste it on a giant slideshow nobody waits for, a stock photo of a handshake, a wall of text, or a cookie banner that covers the whole screen. Every element up top should earn its place by helping the customer decide to call you.

You get three seconds and one small screen. Spend them on the customer, not on decoration.
53% of mobile visitors leave a page that takes more than three seconds to load — before they ever see your offer. (Google)

Above the fold is different on a phone

Here's the trap most local businesses fall into: they design the homepage on a big monitor, it looks fantastic, and they ship it. But your customer isn't on a 27-inch screen. They're standing in a parking lot holding a phone, and the "fold" on that phone is tiny — usually just a headline and maybe one button before they have to scroll.

A desktop hero that looks great can quietly wreck you on mobile. That giant full-width photo with the big centered headline? On a phone it eats the whole screen, and your tap-to-call button gets shoved below the fold where nobody sees it. The customer lands, sees a pretty picture and no obvious way to reach you, and leaves.

So the test that matters is the phone test. The headline and a way to call you have to fit in that first small window without a single scroll. Everything else — the photo, the trust badges, the longer pitch — can wait for the scroll. If you only get the fold right in one place, get it right on the phone. That's where most of your traffic actually is. We go deeper on this in the mobile-first guide.

What "above the fold" is really doing

Step back from the checklist for a second. The fold isn't a design rule for its own sake — it's doing a job. In the first three seconds, a visitor is silently asking three questions:

  • Am I in the right place? Do these people do the thing I need, in the place I live?
  • Can these people actually help me? Are they real, established, and any good?
  • How do I reach them? If I decide yes, what do I do next?

Every one of the seven elements answers one of those questions. Who you are and where you work answer "right place." Your trust signal answers "can they help." Your call button and clear next step answer "how do I reach them." Speed and mobile aren't on the list to be nice — they're there because if the page is slow or broken on a phone, none of the answers ever arrive. Once you see the fold as three questions instead of seven boxes, it gets a lot easier to judge your own.

The fold answers three questions in three seconds. Right place, right people, right here's how to reach us.

The common mistakes that waste the fold

Most local homepages don't fail because they're missing the seven things. They fail because they crowd them out with stuff that doesn't earn its place. The usual suspects:

  • A giant rotating slider. Three or four slides that fade in and out. Nobody waits for slide two. The first slide is all that gets seen, so you've split your one shot into quarters and thrown three away.
  • A stock hero photo with no message. A smiling model in a hard hat, a generic handshake, a city skyline that isn't your city. It looks "professional" and says absolutely nothing about what you do or where.
  • A hidden phone number. Tucked in the top corner in small gray text, or stuffed behind a menu icon. If a customer has to hunt for how to call you, plenty of them just won't.
  • Vague slogans. "Quality you can trust." "Excellence in service." "Your satisfaction is our priority." These are wallpaper. They could belong to any business on earth. Replace them with what you do and where you do it — that's the part a customer is actually scanning for.

How to test your own homepage

You don't need a tool or an audit for this. You need your phone and ten seconds. Do it right now.

Open your homepage on your phone — and turn off wifi first, so you're on cellular, the way a real customer outside their house would be. Let it load. Glance at the screen for about three seconds, the way you'd glance at any link before deciding to stay. Then ask yourself three questions:

  • Do I know what they do?
  • Do I know where they work?
  • Do I know how to contact them without scrolling?

If the answer to any of those is no, that's your highest-value fix — bigger than a new logo, a blog, or a fresh coat of paint on the rest of the site. Fix the fold first. Everything else on your homepage only matters to the people who got past the first three seconds, and the fold is what decides whether they do. The short version

  • Say who you are, what you do, and where — instantly and in plain words
  • Put a call or quote button up top, not in a menu
  • Show one trust signal: rating, license, years, or “family-owned”
  • Make the next step obvious and impossible to miss
  • It has to be fast and right on a phone, or none of it matters
