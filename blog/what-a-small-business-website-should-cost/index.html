Pricing

What a small business website should actually cost in 2026

From free builders to $10k agencies — where the real value sits, and how to avoid both overpaying and buying something that never gets you hired.

Pricing June 11, 2026 · 7 min read · By Wireset Web

Ask five people what a website costs and you'll get five answers between "free" and "ten grand." Both extremes are real, and both can be the wrong call. Here's an honest map of what you're actually paying for at each price.

The price tiers, and what each really buys

  • Free DIY builders. Tempting, and fine for a placeholder. The hidden cost is your time and the ceiling: slow templates, generic design, and a site that looks like ten thousand others. You're the web designer now, on nights and weekends.
  • $500–$1,500, done for you. The sweet spot for most local service businesses: a fast, custom-built, mobile-ready site that you own, without paying for overhead you'll never use. This is where value peaks.
  • $5,000–$10,000+ agencies. Real craft, but you're also funding account managers, sales teams, and offices. Worth it for complex builds; overkill for a plumber who needs to get found and get called.
1 job is what a good site costs most service businesses. One new customer it brings in usually pays for the whole build.

What you should never pay for

Some line items are pure margin for the seller and pure cost for you:

  • Rented access to your own site. Paying a monthly just to keep a site you can never take with you. (We wrote a whole piece on this trap.)
  • Guaranteed SEO rankings. Nobody can promise a #1 spot. Anyone who does is selling you something they don't control.
  • Features you'll never touch. A booking engine, a blog, a members area — great if you'll use them, dead weight if you won't.
The right price isn’t the lowest or the highest. It’s the one that gets you hired and lets you walk away.

The real cost question

Don't ask "what does a website cost?" Ask "what does not having a good one cost?" One slow week, one customer who clicked your competitor because your site took five seconds to load, one job lost to a business that simply looked more credible online. Measured that way, a well-built site is one of the cheapest things a local business buys.

The four ways to get a site built, and what each really costs

Price is downstream of who you hire. There are basically four roads, and each one has a real trade-off behind the sticker.

  • The DIY builder. Wix, Squarespace, GoDaddy. Cheap monthly, no upfront. The catch is twofold: it eats your nights and weekends, and it tends to look like you built it yourself. You're not paying in dollars, you're paying in hours and in a site that doesn't quite earn trust. Fine for a placeholder. Rough as your main pitch to a paying customer.
  • The cousin or freelancer. Someone's nephew "does websites." Could be $200, could be great. The honest risk is reliability: the build drags, then they get a real job and stop answering texts. When something breaks a year later, you're on your own with a half-finished site and no one to call. Cheap is only cheap if it actually gets finished.
  • The big agency. $5,000 to $25,000 and up. You get real designers and project managers, and for a complex build that's money well spent. But you're also funding their office, their sales team, and their account managers. For a local shop that needs to get found and get called, that's a lot of overhead for a four-page site.
  • The productized small studio. Flat price, fixed scope, fast turnaround. You know the number before you start and you own what you get. That's the lane we built Wireset in: $499, $799, or $1,299 one-time plus a low monthly for hosting. Less hand-holding than an agency, far more reliable than a freelancer who might vanish.

No road is wrong for everyone. But for most local service businesses, the productized studio hits the spot the other three miss.

What actually drives the price

When a quote swings from $500 to $5,000, it's not random. A handful of real factors move the number:

  • Number of pages. A tight five-page site costs less than a thirty-page one. Most local businesses need fewer pages than they think.
  • Custom design vs. template. A polished template tuned to your brand is fast and affordable. A fully bespoke design drawn from scratch costs more, and most local shops don't need it.
  • Copywriting. Words that actually sell take time. If you write your own, you save money. If someone writes it for you, that's real work and it shows in the price.
  • Photos. Good photos lift a site. A shoot costs money; decent stock or your own phone photos cost little.
  • Special features. Online booking, e-commerce, a customer login, a payment flow. Each one adds build time. A brochure site that gets you called is far cheaper than a store that takes orders.
  • Who owns it at the end. This one's quiet but huge. A build you own is a one-time cost. A build you rent looks cheaper monthly and costs far more over five years.
Pay once for the build you keep. Never rent the thing your whole business points to.

One-time vs. monthly: what you should and shouldn't rent

Some monthly costs are fair. Others are a leash. The line is simple: pay once for things you own, pay monthly only for ongoing service you actually use.

Good: paying once for the build, then a small monthly for hosting. Hosting is a real, ongoing service — a server keeping your site online, fast, and secure. Twenty or thirty bucks a month for that is fair, and it's the same whether you stay or leave.

Trap: renting the whole site so it vanishes the day you stop paying. You make payments for years and own nothing. Stop, and your site, your pages, sometimes your domain disappear with the vendor. We dug into the five-year math on this in rent vs. own your website, and it's uglier than it looks.

Also worth a hard look: the open-ended "SEO retainer." A few hundred a month, forever, with vague reporting and no finish line. Some are legit. Many are just margin. We laid out how to tell the difference in stop paying monthly for SEO. The on-page SEO that matters most should be built into the site once, not billed to you every month.

What you should get for a fair price

Forget the number for a second. Here's what a fair quote should actually deliver, no matter who you hire:

  • Mobile-fast. It loads quick on a phone on a so-so connection. Most of your customers are on one.
  • You own the files and the domain. Your name on the domain registration. The site files yours to take if you ever leave.
  • Clean on-page SEO. Proper titles, descriptions, headings, and local signals built in — not sold back to you as a monthly add-on.
  • A contact path that works. A click-to-call button and a form that actually lands in your inbox. The whole point is to get you hired.
  • No lock-in. No rented access, no ransom over your own content, no surprise fees to make a change.

Use that as a checklist on any quote you get. If a seller can't say yes to all five in plain English, the price doesn't matter — it's the wrong deal. If they can, you're looking at a fair one, whatever the tier.

The short version

  • Most local service businesses are best served in the $500–$1,500 range
  • Free builders cost you in time, speed, and a generic look
  • Agencies are worth it for complex builds, overkill for getting found and called
  • Never pay for rented access, ranking guarantees, or features you won’t use
  • The real cost to weigh is the jobs a bad site loses you
