Why website speed matters for local SEO

Google measures it. Your customers feel it. And on a phone at a job site, three seconds is all you get.

SEO June 11, 2026 · 6 min read · By Wireset Web

Your website can be beautiful, accurate, and stacked with five-star reviews — and still lose the job because it took four seconds to show up. Speed isn't a technical footnote. It's the first impression you make before a single word is read, and it's one of the few things Google measures about your site directly.

Google decided slow sites should rank lower — and said so out loud

Most of what Google uses to rank websites is a black box. Speed is the exception. Since 2021, Google has used a set of published, real-world measurements called Core Web Vitals as an official ranking signal. Three of them carry the weight:

  • Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) — how long until your main content actually appears. Google considers under 2.5 seconds "good."
  • Interaction to Next Paint (INP) — how fast the page responds when someone taps a button. Good is under 200 milliseconds.
  • Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) — whether the page jumps around while it loads. Good is under 0.1.

You don't need to memorize any of that. The point is simpler: Google is timing your site with a stopwatch, comparing it against your competitors, and quietly using the result to decide who shows up first. When two HVAC companies are otherwise neck and neck, the faster site takes the higher spot.

Speed is the cheapest salesperson you'll ever hire

Ranking is only half the story. Even after someone clicks, every extra second costs you customers. Google's own research found that as a page's load time climbs from one second to three, the chance a visitor gives up and leaves jumps by 32%. Stretch it to five seconds and that probability nearly doubles.

Think about what that means for a service business. You paid — in time, in ads, in years of word of mouth — to earn that tap on your link. A slow site takes that hard-won click and tosses a third of it in the trash before your phone number even finishes loading.

A slow website doesn't just rank lower. It takes the customers you already earned and hands them to whoever loads faster.

Your customers aren't on fast wifi. They're in a truck.

The speed test that matters isn't your office laptop on fiber. It's a homeowner standing in a flooding basement, on a three-year-old phone, with two bars of signal, deciding which plumber to call right now. That's the real world your website lives in.

Heavy sites — the kind page builders and bloated themes crank out — feel fine on a fast connection and fall apart on a slow one. The usual culprits:

  • Giant, uncompressed photos that were never resized for a phone screen
  • A dozen tracking scripts, chat widgets, and pop-ups loading in the background
  • Page-builder code bloat — layers of generated junk to render a simple page
  • Hosting on a single slow server instead of a fast network near your customer

What actually makes a site fast (and why most local sites aren't)

Fast isn't magic. It's discipline — a handful of decisions most agencies skip because cutting corners is cheaper:

  • Hand-coded pages, not page builders. No theme bloat, no plugin pileup — just clean code measured in kilobytes.
  • Images sized for the screen. A photo that looks identical at a fraction of the file size. (The four-megabyte hero photo is the number-one thing slowing down local sites.)
  • Served from the edge. Your site lives on a global network — the same kind of infrastructure behind the internet's biggest names — so it loads fast from every zip code you serve.
  • Nothing that doesn't earn its place. No trackers, no spyware, no autoplay widgets stealing the first second of every visit.

This is exactly why the little badge in our header can honestly say a page loaded in a few dozen milliseconds. It isn't a trick — it's just what's left when you stop shipping junk.

The short version

  • Google measures speed directly (Core Web Vitals) and ranks faster sites higher
  • Over half of mobile visitors leave a page that takes more than 3 seconds
  • Every extra second of load time measurably increases the odds they bounce
  • Your real audience is on phones and slow connections, not office wifi
  • Speed comes from clean code, right-sized images, and edge hosting — not luck

If your site takes more than a couple of seconds to load on a phone, you're not just losing a ranking spot — you're losing the customer who was ready to call. The good news: it's fixable, and it doesn't take a six-week project to fix it.
