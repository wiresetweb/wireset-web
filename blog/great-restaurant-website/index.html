Food

What makes a great restaurant website

Menu, hours, location, order link — above the fold or you’ve lost them. What a hungry, in-a-hurry customer actually needs.

Food June 11, 2026 · 6 min read · By Wireset Web

A restaurant website has the most impatient visitor of all: someone hungry, on their phone, deciding right now where to eat. They want four things instantly — the menu, the hours, the location, and a way to order. Make them hunt and they’re already looking at the next place.

What a restaurant customer is actually looking for

They didn't come to admire your website. They came with a problem and a question: can this business solve it, and can I trust them to? Everything on the page should answer that, fast. The essentials:

  • The menu, front and center — as real text, not a slow PDF or a photo they have to pinch-zoom.
  • Hours and location with a tap-to-call and a map, no scrolling.
  • Order / reservation links — online ordering, delivery, or booking — obvious and one tap away.
  • Real, appetizing photos of your actual food.
  • Fast load — a hungry customer has zero patience.
  • Flawless on mobile, where nearly all of this happens.

The parts most sites in your trade get wrong

Three mistakes show up again and again: burying the phone number, hiding the service area, and a homepage that takes too long to load on the exact device customers use. Fix those three and you're ahead of most of your competition before you've added a single clever feature.

Your customers don’t want a brochure. They want to know you can help, and reach you in one tap.

Menu, hours, location — in that exact order

A hungry person on their phone is running through three questions, in order. What do you serve? Are you open? How do I get there? That’s the whole decision. Build your homepage to answer them in that sequence and you’ve already won most of the battle.

Don’t make any of these a scavenger hunt. Each one should be reachable in a single tap from the top of the page — not buried three menu levels deep, not stuffed at the bottom of an “About” page nobody reads.

  • Menu first. It’s the reason they’re here. Put a link to it in the very top bar, and show your most popular items high on the page.
  • Hours second. Show today’s hours plainly, with a clear “Open now” or “Closed” state if you can.
  • Location third. Address, a tap-to-call number, and a one-tap map link. Don’t make someone copy-paste your address into their maps app.

Everything else — your story, your awards, the chef’s bio — is secondary. Keep it, but keep it below. The order matters because attention runs out fast, and a phone screen only shows so much at once. Speaking of phones: this is all happening on a small screen with spotty signal, so it has to load fast and work with one thumb. We get into that in what your site looks like on one bar of signal.

Your menu should be HTML, not a PDF

A PDF menu feels easy. You already have one for print, so you upload it and call it done. On a phone, it’s a disaster. The customer taps it, waits for a multi-megabyte file to download, then gets a tiny wall of text they have to pinch and drag around to read. Half of them give up before they find the entrées.

A real on-page menu — actual HTML text — fixes all of it at once:

  • It loads instantly. No download, no spinner, no waiting on one bar of signal.
  • It’s readable without zooming. The text reflows to fit the screen, every time.
  • It’s searchable. Someone looking for “gluten free” or “birria” can find it with the phone’s find-on-page.
  • Google can read it. Text in a PDF is mostly invisible to search; text on the page is exactly what gets you found.

You don’t have to choose between pretty and functional. Keep the print PDF for the table, but the website menu should be clean text with prices. Then add a few good photos of your signature dishes — the birria taco that gets ordered ten times a night, the dessert people post. Photos sell; a wall of italic text doesn’t. Take orders and reservations without handing 30% to the apps

The delivery apps are convenient, and they’ll take a brutal cut for the privilege — often 15% to 30% of every order. On thin restaurant margins, that’s most of your profit on that ticket. The apps have their place for discovery, but you should never make them the only way to order from you.

Your own website is where you keep your margin. It doesn’t have to be fancy:

  • A clear “Order by phone” button with a tap-to-call number is the simplest version, and it costs you nothing.
  • An “Order by text” line works great for regulars and pickup orders.
  • A direct online-order button — your own ordering page or a low-fee provider — keeps the whole sale in your pocket.
  • A reservation link that goes straight to your booking, so a table request doesn’t cost you a commission either.

The point isn’t to fight the apps. It’s to give the customer who already found you a direct path that doesn’t skim a third off the top. Loyal regulars especially will happily order direct if you make it one obvious tap. Every order that comes through your own site instead of an app is money you keep.

Win “restaurants near me” and the Friday-night search

Most of your new customers aren’t typing your name. They’re searching “restaurants near me” or “tacos open now” on a Friday night, and Google decides who shows up. Your Google Business Profile is what wins that moment — and it lives or dies on a few details.

  • Accurate hours, always. This matters more than anything else. If Google says “Open now” and you’re dark, that customer drives over, finds a locked door, and never trusts you again. Keep your hours dead-on.
  • Holiday hours. Set special hours for holidays and slow days. A wrong holiday hour is exactly the kind of mistake that loses a hungry family for good.
  • Current photos. Fresh shots of your food and your room. People eat with their eyes before they ever taste anything.
  • Reviews, and replies to them. Ask happy customers to leave one, and reply to the ones you get — good and bad. It signals you’re open, active, and paying attention.

Get your hours right and keep your profile current and you’ll show up exactly when someone two blocks away is deciding where to eat. Most restaurants set their profile once and forget it, which is precisely why the few who keep it sharp clean up. We walk through the common slip-ups in Google Business Profile setup mistakes.

The short version

  • Put the menu up top as real text, not a slow PDF
  • Show hours, location, and a map without scrolling
  • Make ordering or reservations one obvious tap
  • Use real photos of your actual food
  • Be fast and perfect on a phone — hungry people don’t wait
